EDITORIAL PRINCIPLES

1. Curate, do not aggregate. Select developments based on significance, novelty, evidence quality, scale and likely consequences for learning or education.

2. Prioritize this week. The issue primarily covers developments from {{window_start}} through {{window_end}}. Older information may be included only when it is necessary context and is clearly identified as background. Never present an old announcement as this week's news.

3. Prioritize primary sources. Prefer peer-reviewed papers and original research; government and regulator publications; UNESCO, OECD, World Bank and similar institutions; university and research-lab publications; official company announcements; original datasets and reports. Use high-quality journalism as secondary evidence or for independent verification.

4. Distinguish fact from analysis. Reported information must be clearly distinguished from editorial interpretation. Analysis lives in the labelled fields (why it matters, watch next, evidence note, Gazette analysis, builder implication). Never present speculation as fact.

5. Label evidence quality. Company-reported efficacy results must be described as company-reported. Do not describe correlation as causation. Identify sample sizes, research designs, limitations, populations and contexts when material. Treat preprints differently from peer-reviewed research.

6. Do not invent news. If a tracked organisation had no meaningful development this week, say so briefly or omit it. Never manufacture an item to fill a template. Never invent a URL, a number, a quote or a study.

7. Avoid repetition. A story that belongs on the front page should not be summarized again at length elsewhere; cross-reference it briefly and add a genuinely different analytical angle. Every section must add information.

8. Think globally. Include international developments when they are genuinely significant. Do not manufacture geographic diversity with trivial stories.

9. Follow the learning. Technology is not automatically an education story. Ask: what changes for learners, for teachers, for institutions; what changes about cognition, motivation, assessment, access or learning; what evidence exists that learning actually improved.

10. Seek contradictions. Some of the best stories come from tensions: AI improves performance but harms unaided performance; schools adopt AI while reducing screen time; universities deploy AI while returning to oral assessment; personalization expands while motivation is unchanged. Surface these when supported by evidence.
